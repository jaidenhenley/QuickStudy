The Water Cycle

The water cycle describes how water moves between the oceans, the atmosphere, and the land. It has no starting point, because the same water is continually recycled. The total amount of water on Earth stays roughly constant; only its location and form change.

Evaporation is the process in which liquid water becomes water vapor. The sun provides the energy. Most evaporation happens over the oceans, which hold about 97 percent of Earth's water. Plants also release vapor through tiny leaf pores in a process called transpiration. Scientists often combine the two and call the total evapotranspiration.

As water vapor rises, it cools. Cooler air cannot hold as much vapor, so the vapor condenses into tiny droplets around dust and salt particles. Condensation is the step that forms clouds and fog. Condensation releases heat into the atmosphere, which is one reason clouds can power storms.

When droplets in a cloud grow heavy enough, they fall as precipitation. Precipitation takes several forms: rain, snow, sleet, and hail. The form depends on the temperature of the air the droplets pass through on the way down. Snow that settles on mountains can remain frozen for months or even thousands of years in glaciers.

Once water reaches the ground, it follows one of several paths. Runoff is water that flows over the surface into streams, rivers, and eventually the sea. Infiltration is water soaking into the soil. Water that sinks deeper collects as groundwater in layers of rock and sand called aquifers. Groundwater moves slowly, sometimes only a few centimeters per day, and can emerge again at springs.

Collection is the stage in which water gathers in oceans, lakes, and underground reservoirs before the cycle begins again. A drop of water may spend about nine days in the atmosphere, a few weeks in a river, hundreds of years in the deep ocean, and thousands of years in an ice sheet.

Human activity affects the cycle in several ways. Cities replace soil with pavement, which increases runoff and reduces infiltration. Dams slow rivers and store water. Pumping groundwater faster than aquifers refill can lower water tables. A warmer climate increases evaporation, which can make some regions drier and others rainier.

Key terms to remember: evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff, infiltration, groundwater, aquifer, and collection. Understanding how these steps connect explains why droughts, floods, and storms occur, and why protecting watersheds matters for clean drinking water.
